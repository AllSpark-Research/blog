Intelligence in the Wild

How AllSpark studies foundation models and agents through real-world interactions.

Abstract

AllSpark Research studies intelligence as it unfolds beyond the lab — where models and agents perceive, decide, act, and adapt in open environments. We introduce the Field–Instrument–Loop framework for studying real-world interactions, the instrument stack we use to observe and measure behavior, and the feedback loops that drive model evolution. This manifesto sets the agenda for a research program grounded in reality, oriented toward utility, and accountable by design.

Why we study intelligence in the wild

Benchmarks tell us what models can do. The wild shows us what they actually do.

In the wild, goals are ambiguous, contexts shift, tools are imperfect, and consequences matter. It is here that capabilities compound — or collapse. By embedding models and agents in real-world loops, we move from static evaluation to continuous discovery.

"Intelligence is not defined in a vacuum. It is observed, tested, and evolved in the wild."

AllSpark Research

A model that scores 92% on a reasoning benchmark may fail systematically on real community moderation tasks. The failure mode is not random — it follows patterns. Those patterns are research problems. We formulate them, instrument them, and feed them back into training.

Field

The field is the real world where agents operate — messy, partial, and dynamic. We engage with diverse domains and tasks, capturing rich distributions of contexts, constraints, and outcomes.

Observation

We observed three recurring failure modes in long-horizon tool-use tasks: context loss after tool boundaries, goal drift under partial observability, and silent failure when tool outputs are ambiguous. These were not edge cases — they were the median behavior.

The real community interaction data at Xiaohongshu is a field in this sense: hundreds of millions of user–content interactions, each carrying an implicit signal about what the model got right and what it missed. We do not treat this as a corpus. We treat it as a scientific instrument — one that reveals capability gaps that controlled environments cannot surface.

Instrument

Instruments are how we observe and measure. Our stack captures perception, reasoning traces, actions, tool use, and outcomes — with time alignment and provenance.

Harness, Benchmark, Rubric, and LLM Judge are not auxiliary tools. They are the scientific instruments that make model behavior observable, comparable, and reproducible. Without precise instruments, we cannot distinguish genuine capability from benchmark overfitting.

Design Principle

Evaluation rubrics should be executable, not descriptive. A rubric that cannot be implemented as a deterministic scoring procedure is not a rubric — it is a preference. We insist on rubrics that could, in principle, be run by a harness.

Figure 2. The instrument stack from observation to analysis. Each stage introduces structured provenance: what was observed, how it was recorded, which rubric was applied, where the result was stored, and how it was aggregated.

Our current evaluation infrastructure includes over 40 task-specific harnesses, a shared rubric library with 200+ reusable criteria, and an LLM Judge calibration protocol that we validate against human raters on a monthly cycle.

Loop

The loop closes the cycle: insights become hypotheses, hypotheses become experiments, and results feed model and system updates. Models are not static release artifacts — they are participants in a continuous, field-grounded improvement process.

Figure 3. The feedback loop from deployment back to training. Red dot indicates the signal injection point where field observations re-enter the training pipeline as structured feedback. The cycle operates on a weekly cadence.

Agent trajectories from field deployment are not discarded after serving. They are reviewed, annotated, and filtered for training utility. A trajectory that demonstrates successful reasoning under novel constraints is more valuable than a synthetic demonstration — it carries real-world validity that cannot be easily replicated.

"The loop is not a pipeline. It is a scientific posture — a commitment to closing the distance between what we know in the lab and what happens in the world."

AllSpark Research

Our commitments

We commit to three practices that follow from the Field–Instrument–Loop framework:

Publish failure modes. We will not only report improvements. For every method we describe, we will identify the conditions under which it fails and the inputs that break it. This is not humility — it is scientific hygiene.

Share evaluation artifacts. Rubrics, harnesses, and benchmark datasets are research contributions. Where possible, we will release them alongside the methods they evaluate. Evaluation infrastructure that lives only internally is infrastructure that cannot be challenged or improved by the community.

Name open problems explicitly. At the end of every article, we will list the questions that remain open. A research program that cannot name what it does not know has stopped doing research.

Open Problems

Open Problem

How do we measure the quality of an agent's reasoning trace in a way that predicts downstream task success across different domains? Current metrics conflate length with depth and completeness with correctness.

Open Problem

At what granularity should rubrics be defined to remain both precise enough to be executable and general enough to transfer across task families? This is not a philosophical question — it is an engineering constraint.

Open Problem

Can we build evaluation harnesses that remain valid as models improve? Benchmarks saturate. We do not yet have a principled approach to adaptive evaluation that scales with model capability.

These questions guide our current research program. We do not claim they are the right questions — only that they are the ones we cannot currently answer.
